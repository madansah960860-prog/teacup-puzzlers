A fond look back

Choose a decade from the tabs above and a short round of questions will appear, all about the years you lived through. These are not trick questions or obscure ones; they are the warm, familiar facts of their time — the inventions that arrived in the shops, the events the whole country watched, the small changes that crept into everyday life. Click an answer and the round tells you at once whether you have it right, keeping a gentle tally at the foot.

We have deliberately kept to the things that are easy to check and fond to recall: the opening of a famous theme park, the first steps on the Moon, the arrival of the pocket calculator and the compact disc. You will find no questions about song lyrics or film scenes here, only the solid, sunlit facts of the decades themselves.

The 1950s

The 1950s were a decade of bright optimism and new conveniences. Television moved from a novelty to the heart of the living room, color sets began to appear, and a trip to a brand-new theme park in California became the family outing of the age. It was the decade the polio vaccine brought enormous relief to worried parents, and the decade a simple spinning hoop became a craze in every backyard. Our 1950s round gathers a handful of these cheerful milestones.

The 1960s

If the fifties looked forward, the sixties arrived. It was the decade humankind first left footprints on the Moon — a moment so large that most who saw it can still tell you exactly where they were. Closer to home, the Postal Service tidied the nation's mail with a new five-digit code, the first Super Bowl kicked off, and portable music slipped into a pocket on a new kind of tape. Our 1960s questions revisit these shared, era-defining moments.

The 1970s

The 1970s hummed with the first stirrings of the age to come. Two young men founded a computer company in a garage, the nation threw itself a two-hundredth birthday party, and a simple game of electronic table tennis showed living rooms what a video game could be. Calculators shrank to fit a pocket, and a new tape format let families record and watch films at home. Our 1970s round remembers the decade the future quietly began to arrive.

The 1980s

By the 1980s that future was in full swing. A colorful twisting cube baffled and delighted the whole world, a reusable spacecraft rose on a pillar of flame, and a shiny silver disc began to replace the record and the tape. The personal computer found its way onto more and more desks at home, a new channel filled the airwaves with music, and the first mobile telephones — enormous and dear — hinted at the connected world to come. Our 1980s questions tour this bright, busy decade.

How we chose these four decades

We settled on the 1950s through the 1980s for a simple, warm reason: they are the decades our readers remember most vividly and most fondly. These were, for many, the years of growing up, starting out, raising families, and watching the modern world take shape — the years whose details are etched most deeply, from the first television in the house to the first computer on the desk. A question from these decades is far more likely to land on a real memory than one from a time before living recollection.

They are also, happily, decades rich in clear, checkable milestones: famous openings, firsts, and inventions with firm dates attached. That makes them ideal for fair, friendly trivia, free of the fuzziness that creeps into questions about very recent or very distant times. We may add earlier or later decades in time if readers ask for them, but for now these four feel like the warm heart of living memory, and a lovely place to roam.

Checked, and open to correction

Every answer in these rounds is a settled, well-documented fact, and we check each one against reliable references before it joins the collection. Even so, we are only human, and a date or a detail can occasionally slip past us. If you believe one of our answers is wrong, we would be genuinely grateful to hear from you — a short note to our team is all it takes, and we will look into it and put it right if you are correct.

Tip: These rounds are a delight to play with family. Call the questions out at a gathering and let each generation shine on its home ground — the grandparents on the fifties, the grandchildren on the eighties.

The pleasure of remembering together

There is something especially warm about trivia drawn from your own lifetime. Each question is not just a fact to recall but a small door into a memory — the first television in the house, the day of the Moon landing, the Christmas a certain toy appeared under the tree. Playing these rounds is really an invitation to remember, and remembering is one of the gentle joys of a long life well lived.

Share them, then, with the people you love. A grandchild will marvel that you watched the Moon landing live; you will marvel that they have never known a world without a computer in their pocket. Between you lies the whole sweep of these remarkable decades.

Why looking back feels so good

There is a particular warmth in remembering, and it is not mere sentimentality. The things we lived through young are woven into who we are, and brushing against them again — the name of a toy, the year of a first, the arrival of some now-ordinary gadget — brings back not just the fact but the feeling that surrounded it. A question about the first color television is really a question about your own living room, your own family gathered around it, a particular evening long ago. The fact is just the doorway; the memory is the room beyond.

That is why we built these rounds by decade rather than by subject. We wanted each one to feel less like a test and more like a visit to a familiar place — a chance to walk back through the years you know best and find them still there, waiting. Whether you lived through all four decades or only some, each round is an invitation to remember, and remembering, done gently and in good company, is one of the quiet pleasures of a long life.

Questions that start a conversation

One of the loveliest things about trivia drawn from living memory is how readily it turns into talk. Ask someone which theme park opened in 1955 and you will likely get the answer — and then a story: the first time they visited, who took them, what it cost, how the drive seemed to last forever. The question is just a spark; the memories it lights are the real pleasure, and they are different for everyone who answers.

That makes these rounds wonderful for gatherings. Read a question aloud at a family dinner and watch it unlock a half-hour of reminiscing — the gadgets that once seemed miraculous, the news everyone remembers watching, the fads that swept through a neighborhood. Younger relatives often love these stories most of all, for they are a window into a world they never knew. A single good question can do what no photograph album quite manages: it can get the stories flowing all on their own.

A short tour through the gadgets

One of the most striking things about these four decades is how much of modern life arrived during them. The 1950s brought color television into the home and put a vaccine in reach of every worried parent. The 1960s sent people to the Moon and slipped music onto a pocket-sized tape. The 1970s shrank the calculator to fit a pocket, taught the living room to play a video game, and saw two young men found a company in a garage that would change everything.

And the 1980s brought the future into full view: a shiny silver disc that would retire the record, a personal computer that would settle onto desks everywhere, and a telephone — vast and costly though it was — that you could carry out of the house. Taken together, these rounds are a small history of how the world we live in now was built, decade by decade, invention by invention. To have watched it happen is no small thing, and these questions are a fond way to take the measure of it.

Our promise on accuracy

Because these questions touch real events and real dates, we take special care to get them right. Each answer is checked against reliable references before it joins a round, and we favor the firmly established over the fondly misremembered — for memory, lovely as it is, can play tricks, and a popular story is not always an accurate one. Where a date is widely agreed, we use it; where there is genuine uncertainty, we leave the question out rather than risk misleading you.

Still, we are human, and history is vast. If you spot an answer you believe is wrong, please do write to us; a short note to our team is all it takes, and we will look into it with real gratitude and correct it if you are right. We would far rather be put straight by a sharp-eyed reader than leave a mistake standing. A few friendly questions are a lovely way to travel these decades together. When you have toured all four, the riddles offer a different sort of fun.
